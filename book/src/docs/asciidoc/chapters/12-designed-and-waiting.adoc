[#courses]
=== Courses

A book explains, and a reader who wants to know whether they understood has to judge that alone.
Some people learn better from a course: lessons in an order, a quiz after each, exercises to do, and a record at the end that they finished.

Leanpub, where the books already are, hosts courses of that shape.
A quiz is graded in the browser, an exercise is for practice and is not graded, and a learner who passes gets a certificate of completion as a PDF.
A track is a bundle of courses with a certificate for completing all of them.
A course has no start or end date, so a learner takes it at their own pace.

A course is tied to books and to a release.
Its lessons follow the books, its title names the release, and it is updated with the books, from the difference since the last release tag.
Its certificate says that someone finished it.
It is not a credential, and the course does not present it as one.

==== The first track: diagrams

The first track is on diagrams, Draw.io among them.
Diagrams come first because half of what there is to learn about them does not need Nasdanika, or any code, and is worth learning by anyone who draws them.
The track goes from thinking in diagrams, to reading and writing them with a program, to running them, and that is the visual communication continuum of the Draw.io book read as a curriculum.

[cols="2,5",options="header"]
|===
|Course |What it teaches

|Thinking in Diagrams
|No code and no Nasdanika. What a diagram is and who it is for, what it costs, the continuum from a picture to a model and how to choose a point on it, nodes, connections and what they stand for, and visual features. Draw.io is the tool, because it is free and its file is data. From part one of the Draw.io book and the chapter on diagrams of the SDK book.

|Diagrams in Java
|Loading, navigating, selecting, reading and writing properties, following links, creating and changing diagrams, order, and rendering in a browser. Then the Draw.io model and the diagram model: a diagram as a model in a resource set, with semantic elements that point to what it depicts, and layout with ELK. Java 25 and Maven. From part two of the Draw.io book and the books of the Draw.io model, the diagram model and ELK.

|Executable Diagrams
|Behavior bound to elements and wired along connections, and what it did followed on the picture. It joins the track when executable diagrams are released on the SDK: <<opgraph-and-nsml>>.
|===

Executing a diagram is the end of the arc, and it is the point the runtime has not reached.
Until it does, the second course ends with an exercise in which the learner writes a small interpreter: it walks a flow diagram in its order, from the chapter on geometry and order, and calls the code that a property of each element names.
It shows the idea with the API that is released.
The course says that this is an exercise, and not the way the runtime will do it.

==== Exercises are code

Leanpub does not grade exercises.
A repository does: a module per lesson, with tests that fail until the learner makes them pass.
The solutions are built with every release, so that an exercise a release broke fails the build and not the learner.

The first course has no code to write, and its exercises are drawings.
A learner checks a drawing against a list in the lesson.
A learner who has Java can also save the drawing into the exercise module and run its tests, which check it: every connection has a label, every system has an owner.
The second course opens by reading those tests, so that the first program a learner reads is the one that checked their own drawing.

==== Quizzes are about ideas

A quiz asks about an idea, such as why a diagram is data or when a picture is enough, and not about the signature of a method.
An idea holds for several releases, and a question about a signature goes stale without anyone noticing.

Leanpub courses are written in Markua, where a quiz is a block with an id that keeps its identity from one generation of the course to the next.
The books are AsciiDoc.
The first course is written in Markua by hand, with lessons that point to the chapters they follow.
Generating the lessons from the books, through a Markua writer of the document model, is a later step and only if writing them by hand turns out to cost more than the writer.

Leanpub has a service that turns a book into a course with lessons and quizzes.
Its output may be a first draft, and every question in it is reviewed before it is published: a wrong answer in a quiz costs more than a missing question.

==== Where courses live

In a repository of their own in the SDK organization, built on the runtime's bill of materials like Nasdanika Field Notes: the Markua of each course and the exercise modules with their solutions.
A course draws on several books, and its release is the runtime's.

==== Taking part through a course

A course is a way into the project that asks little at the start.

. *Take a course,* and open an issue where a lesson or a book lost you. A place where a learner got stuck is a defect of the book.
. *Write an exercise* and its solution. It is a small piece of code with a test, and it is reviewed like any other.
. *Write quiz questions,* once the ideas of a course are your own.

==== The item

Capability:: A learner takes a course on diagrams, does exercises that a build checks, and gets a certificate of completion; a contributor adds exercises and questions.
Demonstration:: The track on Leanpub and the repository of its exercises, built with every release.
Status:: [.planned]#PLANNED#, when courses are asked for. Thinking in Diagrams first.
Depends on:: The released Draw.io, Draw.io model, diagram model and ELK books for the first two courses; executable diagrams on the SDK for the third.
Value and effort:: The first course needs no code, follows chapters that are written and do not change with the API, and reaches anyone who draws diagrams: the most value for the least effort, and a measure of whether courses are wanted at all before more are made. The second course costs its exercise modules, and their upkeep every release.
How to help:: Take the first course and say where it lost you; write an exercise.

[#lineage-providers]
=== Lineage providers

[.planned]#PLANNED#
The polymorphic user interface chooses a view by asking along a lineage: the class of an element, then its superclasses, nearest first, and the first view found renders the element.
The SDK book shows it with the classes of Ecore.

The lineage is all that the choice needs.
It does not need a class, and the lineage does not have to be a line of specialization.
It is a sequence of things to ask, nearest first, and whoever knows the element provides it.

[cols="1,3,2",options="header"]
|===
|Provider |The sequence |Where it is

|Java
|A class, its superclasses and interfaces, and `Object` last. Generic types the same way.
|In the runtime: `Util.lineage` and `Util.genericLineage`

|Ecore
|The class of a model element and its superclasses.
|In the runtime: `Util.lineage`

|Meta
|A class of Meta and its supertypes. An object of Meta may change its type or have none, and then the lineage is what the object says about itself.
|Planned. Today a vocabulary of Meta gets there through its projection to Ecore
|===

There may be more: the kinds an element is classified with, the roles of whoever asks, the stages of a lifecycle.

The order is already written once.
`Util.lineage` takes a starting point and a function that gives what is more general, and linearizes with C3, so a new provider is a function and not an algorithm.
The order is still the provider's choice, and it depends on the nature of the lineage.
A simple breadth-first walk is another way, and it agrees with C3 where nothing is inherited along two paths.
Where something is, C3 is the more logical of the two: it never asks the general before the specific.
A lineage that is not a hierarchy at all, such as a list of kinds in the order they were assigned, needs neither.
What is missing is the place where a provider is given: a resolver that asks for the lineage of an element, most likely as a capability, where today it computes the lineage of the element's class.

The word is used twice in this book.
The lineage of a waypoint is where an execution has been, and the lineage here is what an element is.
Both are a sequence read nearest first.

==== Is a, and has a

Inheritance and containment are closer than they look.
"`B` extends `A`" is read as "`B` is an `A`", and what it gives is that `B` has the features and operations of `A`.
Containment gives the same by another route.

A virtual machine that runs Linux is a virtual machine.
It can be modeled as `LinuxVM extends VM`.
It can also be modeled as a `VM` that contains an `OS`, with `Linux extends OS`.
In the first model the lineage of the machine says that it runs Linux.
In the second the class of the machine is `VM` whatever it runs, and what it runs is in what it has.

A lineage provider can read it there.
Asked for the lineage of a machine, it answers "a machine with Linux", then "a machine with an operating system", then "a machine", and a view written for machines with Linux is found with no class `LinuxVM` in the model.

This is why it matters for kinds (<<core-nano-models>>).
A kind is "is a" recorded as "has a": classification that is data, that may be one of several, and that may stop applying.
With kinds in the lineage, a kind can choose a view as a class does.

It can also become too complicated.
Each thing an element contains has a lineage of its own, the combinations multiply, and the order between what an element has and what it is becomes a rule that someone must know.
So the lineage of the class stays the default.
Kinds come next, since their order among themselves is already the same linearization.
Containment is read only where a model says so.

[#learning-graphs]
=== Simulation and learning on graphs

[.planned]#PLANNED#, as a demonstration.
In 2025 I wrote a story, https://medium.com/nasdanika/graph-neural-networks-gnn-in-java-0670469114a4[Graph Neural Networks (GNN) in Java], about a graph whose nodes are predictors and exchange messages along their connections.
It ended with "will it actually work and make good predictions? It is to be seen".
Reactive compute graphs, in the table above, are the wiring it needed, and this section is how to find out.

The greatest value is in the picture.
A system or a process is drawn in Draw.io, each element computes what goes out of it from what comes in, and the drawing becomes a simulation.
Learning along connections comes later, and the network inside a node is a model of its own.

[#graph-simulation]
==== Calculators on a diagram

The one who gains is an architect who designs a new solution that has to be wired into an existing architecture, and who may have the history of how the existing systems behave.
An AI architect adding a language model, a search and an agent to a bank's systems is the case of the day.
The question is what the new solution does to what is already there, and what it needs, before it is built.

Each element of the drawing has a calculator: a function from what comes in, such as transactions per second, to what goes out, such as response time, processor and memory, and the load it puts on what it calls.
A calculator may be one of three kinds.

[cols="1,3",options="header"]
|===
|Kind |What it is

|Deterministic
|A formula or a table to look up.

|Random
|Draws from a distribution, so that the graph is run many times, as in Monte Carlo simulation, and the result is a distribution: a 95th percentile of response time, not one number. A seed in the run makes it repeatable.

|Trained
|A regression or a small network, fitted to history or to load tests.
|===

*A calculator from a load test.*
A component is run in isolation, with mocks for what it calls, under loads that vary.
What is recorded trains a calculator, which is then published with the component, for example as a Maven artifact with a classifier, beside its sources and Javadoc.
Two things keep it honest.
It is trained on the component's own time, which spans give as the time of a span less the time of its children, so that the mocks' response times do not leak into it.
And it carries the range of loads it was trained on: a network extrapolates badly, so a simulation that takes a calculator outside its range says so.

*Calculators composed.*
The calculators of the components are combined on the drawing of an architecture, and the graph settles: the load at a component is what its callers send, its response time is its own plus that of what it calls.
Forward is enough for this.
Nothing travels back along a connection, and none of the machinery of learning below is needed.

This meets <<bindings-capabilities>>.
There, configurations of a system are compared by running them under load.
A simulation compares them first, and only those that look worth it are run.

[#neural-model]
==== A network as a model

A dense network with back-propagation and an optimizer is a few hundred lines of plain Java.
That is less than adapting a library to a graph whose nodes pass values to each other, and it stays within the foundation of Java, EMF core and the OpenTelemetry API.
So the network can be a model in Xcore, saved and loaded as any other.

*Layers in the model, and neurons in a view.*
A model of layers has a network, its layers with their sizes and activations, and the weights of each layer.
A neuron as an object of its own does not scale, because what grows is not the neurons but the weights between them: two layers of a thousand neurons each are a million weights, and a weight as an object costs tens of bytes before any list holds it.
Low millions of weights are a few megabytes in arrays.
Low millions of neurons, densely connected, are out of reach either way.
So the model keeps layers and arrays, and a neuron is a view derived from them, for the picture.

*Weights in a binary resource.*
EMF's binary resource writes a many-valued attribute of `double` as one string per value, and an attribute of bytes as a string too (read in the sources of EMF 2.43).
A package's factory can give the binary resource a converter for a data type of its own, so weights are a data type backed by an array of floats, written as raw bytes.
Compression gains little on weights, which are close to random bits, so a resource of a few million weights is as large as its arrays.

*A network to learn from.*
The neuron view is for people who learn how a network works.
A network of a few dozen neurons is drawn as a diagram, each neuron a node, and values travel forward along bindings and corrections travel back.
On a pump, a step is a frame: the color of each neuron is its value, the width of each connection its weight, and corrections move back across the picture one step at a time.
This is where <<designed-and-waiting,Draw.io actions and animation>> and video as a build artifact meet a subject that is hard to see any other way.
An executor runs the neurons of a layer in parallel, but sums of floating point numbers then depend on the order in which they arrive, so a pump is the default and an executor an option.
A neuron as a node with a binding per connection is orders of magnitude slower than a loop over an array, so the same model runs on arrays when it computes and on nodes when it is shown.

==== Four bindings on a connection

Simulation sends values one way.
Learning along connections sends something back.

A node sends its value to another along a binding.
The reverse binding on the same connection carries what the receiver would have wanted instead: a correction.
A connection between two nodes may carry values both ways, so it has four bindings.

[cols="1,3",options="header"]
|===
|Binding |What it carries

|1. Source to destination
|The value the source sends.

|2. Destination to source
|The correction of the value in 1.

|3. Destination to source
|The value the destination sends.

|4. Source to destination
|The correction of the value in 3.
|===

When a correction is a gradient, this is back-propagation.
A receiver says how its error changes with the value it received.
The sender knows how its output depends on its inputs, so it says the same to each of its own senders, and adjusts its own parameters on the way.
Reverse-mode differentiation is message passing on a graph of computations, and it does not matter whether the graph is one network or many nodes, each with a network of its own.

That revises an assessment of 2026-11-10, which said that bindings could run a graph network and not train it.
The limit was in the libraries, which train one model at a time against a target, and not in the wiring.

==== What a node offers

A node does not have to be a network.
It can be a rule, a seasonal average, a model trained elsewhere, or a call to a language model.
What a node can do with a correction depends on what it is, so a node says which of four things it offers.

[cols="1,3,2",options="header"]
|===
|Level |What the node does |For example

|Predict
|Computes its value from its inputs and ignores corrections. Corrections stop here, as they stop at a frozen layer.
|A rule, a pre-trained model

|Learn locally
|Learns from the actual value of what it predicts, which it observes. Its neighbors' values are inputs like any other, and nothing comes back along a connection.
|An ATM that sees what it dispensed

|Propagate a target
|Given what its output should have been, says what its inputs should have been. The 2025 story did this with an optimizer over the inputs. It needs no gradient.
|A node that cannot be differentiated

|Propagate a gradient
|Given how the error changes with its output, says how it changes with each input, and updates its parameters.
|A small network, a linear model, arithmetic
|===

A connection carries the most that both of its ends offer.

==== What training needs that bindings do not do yet

*Corrections are messages, and not state.*
A binding keeps the latest value and drops what was between, which is right for a prediction.
Two receivers that correct one sender must have their corrections added, and the second must not replace the first.
So a reverse binding accumulates, and is reset for each sample.
<<reactive-enterprise>> draws the same line between state and messages.

*A correction must meet the value it corrects.*
If the forward side has moved on to the next sample, a gradient is applied to the wrong values.
The simplest way is one sample at a time on a `Pump`: settle forward and drain, settle backward and drain, update, then the next sample.
Anything less strict needs the sample in the lineage of each value, which <<waypoints>> provides.

*Cycles.*
Neighbors influence each other, so the graph has cycles, and the forward side settles by iterating.
To train through a cycle, either unroll the iterations, or settle the reverse bindings at the point where the forward side settled.
The second is how the first graph neural network was trained, and it requires each step of the forward iteration to bring values closer together, so that the point exists.
The 2025 story's idea of sending a value between the prediction and the actual one, a step toward convergence, is that kind of damping.

*Settling, both ways.*
A change smaller than a threshold, a number of updates, and time are the rules, as they are for any loop of bindings.
<<waypoints>> has them as rules over lineage.

*Shared predictors.*
In the 2025 story, nodes of one type may share a predictor.
Its parameters then receive the sum of the corrections of every node that uses it.
Graph networks in practice share by default, since a predictor of one node learns only from that node's history.

|The signal is weak and noisy, and a result would be doubted. Not a demonstration.

|Agentic systems
|Calls to language models, connected by what one passes to the next
|A critique of an output is passed back to whatever produced it.
|A correction is text, and what is adjusted is a prompt. TextGrad does this (from memory). It needs <<designed-and-waiting,inference>> first.

==== Is it practical

An assessment, as of November 2026.

*Simulation of architectures has been done, on EMF.*
The Palladio Component Model describes components with their demands on resources, composes them into an architecture and simulates it to predict performance, and it is built on EMF (from memory).
Layered queueing networks are the analytic side of the same question.
What is different here is where the calculators come from, load tests and telemetry rather than specifications written by hand, that the drawing is in Draw.io, which an architect already uses, and that it runs in plain Java beside the rest of the runtime.
Read Palladio before the demonstration is designed, for what it learned about composing components.

*Not as a competitor to the libraries that train graph networks.*
PyTorch Geometric and DGL train graphs of millions of nodes on GPUs (from memory).
A graph here is small, from tens to thousands of nodes, and it is a model that exists already: an architecture, a process, a fleet, a ledger.

*What it has that they do not.*
Nodes of different kinds in one graph: arithmetic, a trained model, a rule, a language model.
Every message is a value that can be seen, with a span of its own.
And the graph is the drawing of the system, so a prediction can be shown where the element is drawn.
Where predictions are reviewed by people, which neighbor moved a prediction, and by how much, may matter as much as accuracy.

*Most of the value comes before the hardest part.*
Simulation of a system is forward only.
And in the ATM scenario, neighbors send their history and their predictions.
History is observed, so a node that predicts from its own history and its neighbors' learns locally, and nothing has to travel back along a connection.
Predictions of neighbors as inputs are stacking: train the neighbors first.
Corrections along connections are needed only when what nodes send to each other is itself learned.
So a demonstration answers the question of the 2025 story, whether the graph helps, before it builds the four bindings.

*Whether it helps is a question of data.*
The demonstration measures it against the same node with no neighbors.
On data where the graph does not help, a graph network is a cost.

*No library is needed.*
<<neural-model>> says why.
It also leaves aside the question of Smile's license, which changed after version 2 (from memory, to be checked).
A library can be a provider of a node later.

==== A demonstration, step by step

. *Calculators of formulas and tables on a drawing.*
An architecture drawn in Draw.io, the International Banking System's or a part of it, with a deterministic or random calculator on each component.
A load is set at the entry, the graph settles, and response time and capacity are shown on the drawing, as a distribution where calculators are random.
It needs only the bindings and waypoints that exist.
. *Calculators from load tests, whose answer is known.*
A few components that are themselves simulated, with queues and limits, so that how they behave together is known.
Each is load tested alone with mocks, its own time is taken from spans, and a calculator is trained on it with the network of <<neural-model>>.
The calculators are composed on the drawing, and the prediction is compared with the components run together.
This step decides.
If calculators trained alone do not predict the composition within a tolerance, inside their ranges, the next steps wait.
. *A new solution wired in.*
A component that calls a language model, with its latency and cost as a random calculator, is added to the drawing, and the simulation says what it does to the components it calls and what it needs.
This is the architect's question, and the story that answers the 2025 one.
. *The network to learn from.*
A small network drawn as neurons, forward and back, a step per frame, as a picture and as a video.
. *Four bindings.*
What nodes send to each other is learned, with gradients along the reverse bindings, one sample at a time on a pump, and then through cycles, on data where neighbors matter, such as a synthetic fleet of ATMs.
It is compared with nodes that learn locally.
. *Nodes of other kinds.*
A rule that propagates targets, and a call to a language model whose correction is a critique.

==== The item

Capability:: A system or a process drawn in Draw.io becomes a simulation: each element has a calculator, deterministic, random or trained, and the drawing shows what a load does to it. Then a network as a model, shown neuron by neuron for learning, and graphs that learn along their connections.
Demonstration:: Components simulated with a known behavior, load tested alone, their calculators composed on a drawing, with a test that the composition predicts the components run together within a tolerance. Then a new solution wired in, and a story that answers the 2025 one.
Status:: [.planned]#PLANNED#, after the first practical graph of reactive compute graphs. Three items: simulation, the network model, and learning along connections.
Depends on:: `runtime.graph`, `runtime.waypoint`, and the Draw.io model. Values on the drawing also on status on diagrams. Running systems also on live telemetry. Nodes that call a language model also on `runtime.inference`.
Value and effort:: Simulation has the most value: it answers a question an architect has before building, on a drawing the architect already has. It is small for formulas and tables, and moderate with trained calculators, most of which is the simulated components and their load tests. The network model is small, and its neuron view is a teaching tool. Learning along connections is the most effort and the least value, and comes last. None is on the path of the next release.
How to help:: Bring a drawing of a system with load test results or history for some of its components, and say what is predicted today and how.
